The download finished an hour ago and Windows still wants to restart. That is
not Windows being slow or obstructive, and the reason is a design decision that
goes back decades. More desktop guides are in
[Windows & Mac](/topics/windows-mac/). For another case where the system holds
on to something you thought was dealt with, read
[deleting files did not free any disk space](/deleting-files-not-freeing-disk-space/),
and for the same wearing-out question on a handset,
[why a phone slows down as it gets older](/phone-slows-down-gets-older/).

## Windows will not let go of a file in use

Open files can prevent replacement, depending on sharing permissions and the
operation involved. Windows does not impose the same unconditional lock on
every open file. Microsoft's documentation distinguishes sharing violations
and deletion pending the closure of handles. Components in use can still
require work to be deferred until a restart.

The consequence is simple and inconvenient. If an update needs to replace a
system library, and something is using that library, the replacement cannot
happen. On a running desktop almost every core system file is in use by
something, often by several things at once.

## So the work is split in two

The part that can happen while you work does. Windows downloads the packages,
verifies them, and stages the new files alongside the old ones. None of this
touches anything in use, which is why your machine keeps running normally and
why the progress bar reaching 100% changes nothing you can see.

The part that cannot wait for a quiet moment gets one made for it. During a
restart there is a window after your programs have closed and before the desktop
and its services start, when very little holds a lock on anything. The component
that applies updates runs in that window and swaps the staged files in.

<Callout type="danger" title="This is the one time not to pull the plug">
  The update screen is the moment when some files are new and some are still old. A forced
  power-off part way through is the common route to a machine that needs recovery media to
  boot.
</Callout>

## Why the restart itself is slow

Nothing is being downloaded during that screen. The time goes on file operations:
replacing components, updating the registry, and rebuilding caches that depend on
what just changed. That work is limited by your disk, which is why the same
update finishes in a minute on an SSD and takes far longer on a mechanical drive.

It is also why the percentage figure moves unevenly. It tracks stages of work,
not bytes, and some stages are much larger than others.

## What this means in practice

Restart on your own schedule rather than waiting to be told. Once the staging is
done, the pending work is sitting on disk and the only thing between you and a
finished update is a restart you control.

If a machine is slow to come back repeatedly, the disk is usually the thing to
look at rather than the update.
