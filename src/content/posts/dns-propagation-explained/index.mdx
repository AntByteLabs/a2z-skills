A DNS change can be visible through one resolver while another still returns
the old answer. The wait involves authoritative publication and independent
caches, rather than one global progress counter. More hosting guides are in
[Websites & Hosting](/topics/websites-hosting/). A concrete case where this
bites is
[Cloudflare Workers custom domain not working](/cloudflare-workers-custom-domain-not-working/),
and the hostname also matters when
[self-hosting n8n on Coolify](/self-host-n8n-coolify/).

<Callout type="note" title="Correction, 27 October 2026">
  The earlier short answer promised an immediate authoritative update and treated TTL as the
  whole wait. Publication delays, delegation and exceptional caching policies also matter; a
  TTL calculation is not a global completion guarantee.
</Callout>

## Authoritative publication comes before cache refresh

Your zone lives on an authoritative nameserver. The authoritative service publishes the records. Once an update has reached
that service, a direct query can show the new value without relying on a
recursive resolver. Providers can use several authoritative servers, so their
own update process also matters.

Almost nobody queries it directly. A visitor's device asks a recursive resolver,
usually run by their internet provider or by a public service. That resolver
asked your nameserver at some point in the past, kept the answer, and will keep
serving it until it decides the answer is too old to trust.

## TTL controls cached record answers

Every record carries a time to live, measured in seconds. It is an instruction
to every resolver that caches the record: keep this for no longer than this
many seconds. A record with a TTL of 86400 may be served from cache for a full
day after it was fetched.

This produces the part that confuses people most. The TTL that governs your wait
is the one that was published before you made the change, because that is the
value resolvers were given when they cached the old answer. Lowering the TTL at
the same time as the change does nothing for anyone who already has a copy.

<Callout type="tip" title="Lower the TTL in advance">
  A lower TTL affects answers fetched after it is published. Existing cached answers retain
  their earlier expiry, so a lower TTL needs time to take effect before a planned move.
  Delegation, provider updates and other caches can still affect the result.
</Callout>

## Why two people see different sites

Each resolver fetched your record at its own moment, so each one expires at its
own moment. During a change the internet genuinely holds two answers at once,
and which one a given visitor gets depends on which resolver they use and when
that resolver last asked.

This is also why a propagation checker is of limited help. It queries a list of
resolvers and reports what each returns. A mixed result can reflect
cached answers or inconsistent authoritative data. It does not prove that the
change was configured correctly or reveal when the last cache will refresh.

## A TTL example, and two limits

Consider a hypothetical resolver that fetched a record at 12:00 with a TTL of
3,600 seconds. The record changes at 12:10. If the resolver uses its existing
cached answer until ordinary expiry, that old answer can remain in use until
13:00. A resolver that first asks after the update may already see the new
value. This is an illustration of the clock, not a measured cutover guarantee.

A missing name can also be cached. [RFC 2308](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2308)
describes negative caching: a previous answer that a name does not exist can
persist after the record has been created. That is different from caching an
old address, and it helps explain why a newly added hostname can appear absent.

Ordinary expiry is not the only policy in use. [RFC 8767](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8767)
describes serving stale DNS data in particular failure conditions to preserve
resolution. That exception is another reason a TTL calculation cannot promise
the exact moment every visitor sees a change.

## Nameserver changes involve separate delegation records

Changing the records inside your zone is governed by your own TTLs. Changing
which nameservers are authoritative is not. That delegation is held by the
registry for your top level domain, it carries its own TTL, and that TTL is
not controlled by changing the TTL of an A record inside your zone.

That is the case worth planning around. Keep the old host serving the site until
the delegation has fully turned over, because the visitors still being sent to
it are real and will see whatever is there.

## What to check when it seems stuck

Query your authoritative nameserver directly first. If it returns the new value,
compare it with the answers seen by affected visitors. A correct DNS answer
alone does not prove that the certificate, web server or application is working. If it returns the old
value, possible causes include an unsaved change, the wrong authoritative server
or publication still in progress. Check the provider’s status before assuming
that either waiting or repeating the edit will resolve it.
