The word propagation describes something DNS does not do. Nothing travels
outward from your domain, and no update is working its way around the world
while you wait. More hosting guides are in
[Websites & Hosting](/topics/websites-hosting/). A concrete case where this
bites is
[Cloudflare Workers custom domain not working](/cloudflare-workers-custom-domain-not-working/),
and the same pattern on a laptop is in
[deleting files did not free any disk space](/deleting-files-not-freeing-disk-space/).

## There is one copy, and then there are caches

Your zone lives on an authoritative nameserver. That server is the only thing
that knows the truth about your domain, and when you edit a record there, the
truth changes immediately. Query it directly and you get the new answer at once.

Almost nobody queries it directly. A visitor's device asks a recursive resolver,
usually run by their internet provider or by a public service. That resolver
asked your nameserver at some point in the past, kept the answer, and will keep
serving it until it decides the answer is too old to trust.

## TTL is the whole mechanism

Every record carries a time to live, measured in seconds. It is an instruction
to every resolver that caches the record: keep this for no longer than this
many seconds. A record with a TTL of 86400 may be served from cache for a full
day after it was fetched.

This produces the part that confuses people most. The TTL that governs your wait
is the one that was published before you made the change, because that is the
value resolvers were given when they cached the old answer. Lowering the TTL at
the same time as the change does nothing for anyone who already has a copy.

<Callout type="tip" title="Lower the TTL in advance">
  Drop the TTL to a few minutes a day before a planned move, wait for the old TTL to expire,
  then make the change. The cutover is then minutes for everyone instead of hours for most
  people.
</Callout>

## Why two people see different sites

Each resolver fetched your record at its own moment, so each one expires at its
own moment. During a change the internet genuinely holds two answers at once,
and which one a given visitor gets depends on which resolver they use and when
that resolver last asked.

This is also why a propagation checker is of limited help. It queries a list of
resolvers and reports what each returns. A mixed result tells you the change is
in progress, which you already knew, and tells you nothing about when the last
cache will expire.

## Nameserver changes are the slow case

Changing the records inside your zone is governed by your own TTLs. Changing
which nameservers are authoritative is not. That delegation is held by the
registry for your top level domain, it carries its own TTL, and that TTL is
typically far longer than anything you would set on an A record.

That is the case worth planning around. Keep the old host serving the site until
the delegation has fully turned over, because the visitors still being sent to
it are real and will see whatever is there.

## What to check when it seems stuck

Query your authoritative nameserver directly first. If it returns the new value,
your work is done and everything after that is waiting. If it returns the old
value, the change did not save, and no amount of waiting will fix it.
