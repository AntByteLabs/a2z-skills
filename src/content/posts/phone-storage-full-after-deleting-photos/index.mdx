Deleting photos changes the library you see, but that count does not explain the
phone's storage total. Before removing anything else, identify whether the
warning concerns the device or its cloud account. They are different limits.

More handset explanations are in [Phones](/topics/phones/). If the phone also
feels slow, [storage pressure is one possible contributor](/phone-slows-down-gets-older/).
The desktop version of the question is
[why deleting files may not release the space you expected](/deleting-files-not-freeing-disk-space/).

<Callout type="note" title="Correction, 26 October 2026">
  This article previously described three copies as a universal explanation and gave a
  60-day Google Photos retention period. Neither is a reliable rule for every device.
  Google's current deletion documentation states 30 days.
</Callout>

## Device storage and cloud storage are different

A full cloud account and a full handset need different diagnoses. Apple
[distinguishes iCloud capacity from device capacity](https://support.apple.com/en-ie/102670):
paying for more online space does not enlarge the storage chip in an iPhone.
A similar distinction matters when a Google account reports its own limit.

The useful question is which category changed after deletion. A photo count
alone cannot show whether photos, downloaded video, messages or another app
accounts for most of the local usage. Deleting more photographs may leave the
largest category untouched.

## A deleted item can remain recoverable

A recently deleted area allows recovery before permanent removal. The retention
period depends on the service. The
[current Google Photos deletion policy](https://support.google.com/photos/answer/6128858?hl=en)
states 30 days. It also explains where deletions affect backed-up devices and
where copies can remain.

A recoverable cloud item is not proof that the same full-size file still occupies
the same amount of space on the handset. The app, backup state and location of
the original all matter. A deletion count and a storage estimate therefore need
to be interpreted together.

## An optimised photo may already use little local space

Apple's [photo-storage explanation](https://support.apple.com/en-ie/105061)
describes keeping originals in iCloud while optimising the copies on the device.
Deleting an item whose original is already online can free less local space than
its original file size suggests.

That is why the number of photographs removed is a poor estimate of the space
recovered. A library of similar-looking thumbnails can contain very different
amounts of local data. It does not follow that the phone secretly holds three
complete copies of every picture.

## Removing a device copy is different from deleting the library item

Google describes [freeing device space from safely backed-up photos](https://support.google.com/photos/answer/6128843?hl=en)
as a separate feature. The pictures can remain visible in the online library
after their local copies have gone. Some recent device copies may be retained.

<Callout type="warning" title="Check what will be removed">
  A normal deletion can propagate through a synced library. A cloud library is not an
  independent backup against every deletion. Keep a separate recoverable copy of anything
  irreplaceable before using permanent removal.
</Callout>

This distinction also explains why a photo can still be visible after local
space has been released. Seeing its thumbnail does not establish where the
original now lives or whether it is available offline.

## Photos may not be the largest remaining category

Google's [Android storage guidance](https://support.google.com/android/answer/7431795?hl=en)
separates downloaded content, apps and temporary data. A gallery is only one
part of that storage. Clearing an app's cache and clearing its data have different
consequences, so they should not be treated as interchangeable repairs.
