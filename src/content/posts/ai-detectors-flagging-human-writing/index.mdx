A detector can flag human writing. That is a false positive: the classification
and the origin of the document disagree. The useful question is how often that
happens for the tool and kind of writing being assessed.

More explanations are in [AI tools](/topics/ai-tools/). Related questions include
[why ChatGPT responses vary](/chatgpt-different-answers-same-question/) and
[why an automated check can finish successfully without proving its intended outcome](/automations-failing-silently/).

## A score needs an evaluation context

A detector compares features of submitted text with patterns learned from
examples. Different tools use different classifiers and thresholds. Some scores
describe a share of passages classified as AI-like; others are presented in a
different form. The number should be read using that product's definition.

A score by itself does not supply the history of a document. It does not show
the author's notes, revisions or sources. Treating it as a direct observation of
authorship gives it a meaning that the test has not established.

## What a study found about non-native writing

[Liang and colleagues' 2023 study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02819)
evaluated detectors using writing samples from native and non-native English
writers. It found a substantial bias against the non-native samples in that
evaluation. The paper also examined how changes to generated text affected
detection.

That is a reason to inspect a tool's evidence before using its score against a
writer. It is not a current false-positive rate for every detector. The study's
tools, samples and date are part of the finding.

The previous version of this article attributed detector failures universally
to predictable sentences and said vendors could not fix the problem. Those
statements went beyond the evidence. A study can establish a problem in its
test conditions without proving that all future classifiers behave alike.

## Newer evaluations can reach different results

[Epoch AI's detector evaluation](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/ai-detectors-false-negatives)
compares human writing with generated writing under different prompting
conditions. Its results illustrate why the detector, input population and
generation method belong beside any performance claim.

False negatives matter as well. A tool can miss generated text while rarely
flagging a particular human sample. It can also perform differently on another
sample. A single headline accuracy figure hides those separate questions.

When comparing evaluations, look for the kinds of documents tested and the
decision threshold. An assessment using long edited articles need not describe
performance on a short classroom response. That difference should be acknowledged
before a score is used in a decision.

## A false-positive rate is not the chance a flagged writer cheated

How many submissions actually contain prohibited material also affects what
a positive result means. Even a fairly small error rate can create disputed
cases in a collection containing mostly human work. The consequences deserve
more attention when the result could affect someone's education or employment.

This does not mean every flag is false. It means that a classification needs
supporting evidence and a process for examining mistakes. Running the same
document through several tools does not automatically create independent proof.

## Review the writing process as well as the output

A fair review can consider drafts, notes, version history and the writer's
account of their sources. Those records provide context a detector score does
not contain. They also have limits and should not be described as impossible to
fake.

For the person making a decision, the important distinction is between a signal
that merits review and a finding established by evidence. A detector can
contribute to the first without settling the second.
